# Telling a Yuma video

This guidance is distilled from previous promo videos and the review rounds they went through. Treat it as the current house style, and update it when brand owners refine it.

## Structure: SCQA

Build the story with SCQA — Situation, Complication, Question, Answer — and let each part become one or more scenes. The viewer should be able to say the whole argument back in one sentence: *given this situation, this complication makes the old way fail, so the question is X, and the answer is this.*

| Part | What it does | In the video |
|---|---|---|
| **Situation** | The world as the viewer already knows it. Nothing to argue with. | The title, and the first half of the opening statement: the familiar way, stated plainly. |
| **Complication** | What changed, or what no longer works. | The familiar way is struck through. One beat, no explanation yet. |
| **Question** | What the viewer now wants to know. Usually left unspoken. | The cut itself: the replacement line lands and the video turns to showing. |
| **Answer** | The subject of the video, and why it resolves the complication. | Everything that follows, in the order below. |

The Answer is the longest part. Lay it out as:

1. **Show it working** — the subject doing its thing, one capability per beat, each beat with one caption. Start with the capability that answers the complication most directly.
2. **How it is made** (short, optional) — the parts and how they fit. Fast, list-like scenes. Leave it out for a non-technical audience.
3. **The real thing** — real screenshots, photos or footage, so the viewer knows it exists.
4. **Closing statement** — the same struck-through device as the opening, one level up: what this is *not*, and what it *is*.
5. **Outro** — on the Forest Green Yuma background: the name, the one action to take, "Powered by Yuma".

Open and close with the same rhetorical device; the symmetry makes the closing land.

A worked skeleton, for a scheduling tool:

- *Situation and complication:* "Planning the week takes a meeting." struck through, replaced by "The week plans itself."
- *Answer, shown:* a calendar fills as requests arrive; two requests collide and are resolved; a person overrides one choice and the rest adjust.
- *The real thing:* the product's own screen.
- *Closing statement:* "A calendar." struck through, replaced by "A planner."
- *Outro:* name, "Try it with your team", Powered by Yuma.

### Know the audience first

The same subject gives different videos for different viewers. Decide who watches before writing the Situation, because the Situation must be *their* world.

- **A buyer or a business audience** — the complication is a cost, a delay or a risk they already feel; the answer is shown as outcomes; leave out "how it is made"; the action is a conversation ("Talk to us").
- **A practitioner or technical audience** — the complication is a limit of the tools they use today; "how it is made" earns its place; the action is something to try (a command, a link).
- **Colleagues** — the situation is shared context, so it can be one line; go straight to what happens and what to do.

### Scaling it down

The full structure is for a video of one to two minutes. For a short piece — an announcement, an event, a teaser of 15 to 30 seconds — keep four scenes: **title, statement, one "show it" beat, outro**. That is still SCQA: the title and statement carry the situation and the complication, the single beat is the answer. Drop "how it is made", the screenshots and the closing statement. One idea, three or four scenes, four or five narration lines.

When the subject is not a product, the same rules hold with the nouns changed: the title says what it is and when, the statement says why it matters, the one beat shows what happens, the outro says what to do.

## On-screen wording

- **One idea per screen.** A caption is one short sentence the eye reads in two seconds.
- **Headlines state the takeaway**, not the topic: *Set it up in one file. No code.* rather than *Configuration*.
- **Contrast beats description.** Strike the old idea, state the new one. *A calendar.* → *A planner.*
- **Present tense, active voice, concrete nouns.** *A colleague joins from their phone, and answers.*
- **Affirm what it is.** Prefer *The plan adapts* to *No fixed plan*. When a negation is needed, make it precise: *No plan to redraw*, not *No plan*.
- **Claim what exists.** Say what the subject does today. Do not present it as a promise, and do not undersell something real as merely a plan. Use no figure that is not a real, sourced one.
- **Name people as participants.** When people appear alongside software, draw them among the other elements, never off to the side as an audience, and mark who is a person with one consistent visual sign and a legend.
- Short lists are fine in the "how it is made" scenes; elsewhere prefer a sentence.

## The Yuma voice

Editorial, spacious and confident, like the Yuma presentation style: light serif headlines, quiet body text, generous space, restraint with effects.

- Confident without superlatives. No "revolutionary", "seamless", "unlock".
- Warm and plain. Short sentences. Let a pause do the work of an exclamation mark.
- Narration complements the picture; it does not read the caption aloud word for word when a shorter spoken line is clearer.
- Narration is tighter than on-screen text, because speech is slower than reading. If the caption says *One late reply never holds up the rest of the team*, the voice can say *One late reply never holds up the team.*
- End on the brand. For a product made by Yuma, the outro says *Powered by Yuma* — on screen or in the last narration line. When the subject is Yuma itself (an internal event, a team announcement), the Yuma logo is enough; do not say Yuma powers Yuma.

## Product vocabulary

Agree the vocabulary with the user before writing, and apply it everywhere — screen, captions, narration, legend. When the subject has a coined name or term, decide explicitly where it replaces the common word and where the common word must stay (an opening line that addresses newcomers usually keeps the common word). Coined names also need a pronunciation check for the voice-over: see `audio.md`.
